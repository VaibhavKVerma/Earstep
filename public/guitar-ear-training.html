Guitar ear training

By Vaibhav Verma · Updated 14 September 2026

If you want to learn guitar and figure out songs without living in tabs, the loop is short: hear the note, name it, then put a finger where that sound lives.

Start guitar ear training

Shapes are not hearing

You can memorize open chords and still not know what you are playing. The hand learns a picture. The ear is not invited. That is why a song in a new key feels like starting over, and why a riff you “know” collapses when someone asks you to play it from silence.

Guitar ear training is the missing half. You do not need every scale on day one. You need C versus D until they are obvious, then the next note, then the habit of finding that note on the neck.

How the neck is drawn

Earstep shows the fretboard the way tabs do: high e on top, then B, G, D, A, and low E at the bottom. Open strings are the first column. Frets count to the right. Thick strings sit lower on the screen because they sit lower on a guitar in playing position when you look down at a tab.

Standard tuning from low to high is E A D G B E. The same letters appear twice because the first and sixth strings are both E, two octaves apart. When you tap a fret in Show me on guitar, Earstep plays that pitch so the picture and the sound stay tied together.

One sound, several frets

Piano maps each pitch to one key. Guitar repeats pitches. A G can live on more than one string. That is not a trick question. If the task is “which note?”, any G is the name. If the task is “this exact pitch,” only the matching octave counts. Earstep lets you switch that rule on the Find it on guitar screen.

Use the repeats as a feature. Once you can name the note, hunt a second place it lives. That is how you later move a melody into an easier position instead of stretching for the first fret you remember.

Open strings as anchors

The six open names are a map you already own if you tune the guitar: E, A, D, G, B, e. When you hear an open G, you are hearing the third string unfretted. When you hear a note a whole step above that G, you are in A territory on that string. Anchors beat abstract theory when you are holding wood and wire.

The hear → name → find loop

Earstep plays acoustic guitar, electric guitar, piano, or a simple tone. Name the letter. Then open Find it on guitar or Hear → Sing → Find. Humming is for you, not a score. The guitar tap is the check: did the sound in your head land on a real fret?

Do this with public-domain phrases next — Twinkle, simple sargam lines — so the notes arrive in a tune instead of as isolated beeps. Isolated notes build the dictionary. Phrases build sentences.

Guitar and piano are the same names

C on guitar and C on piano are the same pitch class. The difference is geography. Piano is a straight line of keys. Guitar is a grid. Ear training for the names transfers. Finding the note is the guitar-specific extra, and it is why this site draws a fretboard instead of only multiple-choice letters.

A first week on the instrument

Day 1–2: C and D only, guitar sound, name the letter. Day 3: find those letters on the neck after you hear them. Day 4: add E. Day 5: one Low / Middle / High round so height does not ambush you. Day 6–7: a short melody and one song phrase. Keep sessions short enough that you still want tomorrow.
