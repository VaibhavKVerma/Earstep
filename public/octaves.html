Octaves and pitch height

By Vaibhav Verma · Updated 14 September 2026

C3 and C4 are both C. They do not sit at the same height. Mixing those two facts is why beginners answer “high” when the question was “which letter?”

Practice low / middle / high

Two jobs, two drills

Pitch class is the letter (or Sa, or Do). Octave is the register — how high that letter is sung or played. If you try to learn both in one multiple-choice tap, the letter never sticks. Earstep splits them. First you name C versus D even if the guitar plays a high C. Later you answer Low, Middle, or High as its own question.

Middle C (C4) is a useful landmark on piano. On guitar the same letter appears in several places. A “high C” on one neck is a “middle C” feeling on another voicing. The words Low, Middle, and High in the trainer are relative to the notes it just played, not a claim about concert pitch exams.

Why octaves sound “the same but not”

Double the vibration rate and you get a pitch that shares a letter and feels like a copy higher up. Harmony uses that. Melodies jump octaves to stay singable. Your ear already knows the family resemblance. The work is noticing the jump without losing the name.

Practice one letter across heights: hear C2, C3, C4, C5 and keep answering C. Then flip the drill: keep the letter hidden and only choose the height. That second drill is the One note, many pitches mode and the Low / Middle / High mode.

Guitar: same name, other fret

Because the neck repeats pitches, a matching letter can be the wrong octave. If the trainer asks for the exact pitch, a G on a different string may be the right name and the wrong height. Use the “exact pitch” checkbox when you want that stricter game. Leave it off when you are still locking letters.

When height starts to matter for songs

Once you can name the notes of a short phrase, height tells you where to put it on the guitar so it is comfortable. A melody that is all “high” on a thin string might sit better an octave down. That choice is musical, not a test. The ear has to hear the register before the hand can move it.
