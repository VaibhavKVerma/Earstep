Learn musical notes by ear

By Vaibhav Verma · Updated 14 September 2026

Want to learn notes, not just read them? A note is a named sound. Earstep plays the sound. You give it the name.

Learn notes now

The seven natural names

Western music reuses seven letters: C, D, E, F, G, A, B. After B the sequence starts again one octave higher. Those seven are the white-key names on a piano and the first set most guitar methods mention before sharps and flats. Sharps can wait. If the seven naturals are shaky, twelve names will not save you.

The steps are not all the same size. C to D, D to E, F to G, G to A, and A to B are whole steps. E to F and B to C are half steps — they sit closer. That is why F can feel sneakier after E, and why “just go up one letter” is not always the same distance in sound.

Start with C and D

C and D are neighbors with a clear whole-step gap. They show up in beginner songs. They are easy to find on guitar once you know an open D or the third-fret G shape’s neighborhood. When you can tell them apart without a visual cue, add E. C–D–E is the first three notes of a C major scale and a staircase you can hum: low, middle, high.

Do not collect letters for the sake of a complete set. Add F when E is stable. Add G when F is stable. G is also an open string, so your ear and your hand meet there often.

Same letter, different height

C2, C3, C4, and C5 are all C. The number is the octave. C4 is the C nearest the middle of a piano, often called middle C. On guitar, the same letter appears in several positions. Learn the name first. Then practice “which C?” as its own drill so a high note does not make you abandon the letter you already knew.

Three ways to say the same scale

  • Western: C D E F G A B
  • Solfège: Do Re Mi Fa Sol La Ti
  • Sargam: Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni

If Sa is C, then C is Sa, D is Re, E is Ga, F is Ma. Move Sa to D and the relationships stay — the melody just sits in a new key. Earstep can show Western letters, movable solfège, or sargam from Settings. The listening work does not change. Only the labels on the buttons change.

What you do not need yet

You do not need to read five-line staff notation to start. You do not need interval quality names on day one. You do not need a tuner app shouting cents at you while you try to hear C versus D. Those tools have a place. They are not the first place.

You also do not need to be “musical.” If you can tell a low sound from a high one, you can learn note names the same way you learned spoken words: a few at a time, with lots of listening.

A simple order

Letters, then octaves, then guitar location, then distance between two notes, then a short melody. That is the Earstep lesson list and the Journey map. Skip a lesson if you already know it. Lessons are a path, not a lock.
