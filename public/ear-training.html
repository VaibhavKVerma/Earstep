Ear training for beginners

By Vaibhav Verma · Updated 14 September 2026

Ear training means you hear a sound and know what it is. You do not need perfect pitch. You need a small set of notes, honest listening, and enough repeats that guessing fades.

What ear training actually trains

Music is vibration. A string, a voice, or a piano hammer makes air move faster or slower. Faster motion sounds higher. Slower motion sounds lower. Musicians pin names onto the pitches they use most: C, D, E, and the rest. Ear training is learning those names from sound instead of from a diagram.

That is different from learning to read notation. Reading is useful. It is not the same skill as hearing a C and knowing it is a C. Plenty of players can follow a tab and still cannot tell whether the next note went up or down. Earstep is for the listening half.

Why “all twelve notes on day one” fails

Many ear trainers throw every chromatic pitch at you immediately. The session feels impressive and the accuracy stays near chance. You are not slow. The set is too large. Spoken language was not taught that way either: you did not learn every word in the dictionary on the first afternoon.

Two notes are enough. C is a little lower. D is one step higher. Close your eyes if the screen pulls attention. Replay the sound. Name it. When those two stop feeling like a coin flip, add E. That staircase — low, middle, high — is the start of a major scale and the start of most songs you already know.

Pitch class first, height second

The same letter can live in several places. C3 is lower than C4. Both are still C. The letter is the pitch class. The number is the octave. Mixing those jobs is the most common beginner tangle: you hear a high C, you answer “high,” and you never lock the name.

Earstep splits the work. First you name the letter, even if the guitar plays it in a different octave than you expected. Later you practice Low, Middle, and High as its own game. Then you put both together when you walk a short public-domain song.

Relative pitch is the everyday skill

Absolute pitch — naming any isolated note with no reference — is rare and not required to play guitar or to figure out a melody. Relative pitch is the daily tool: you hear one sound, then the next, and you know whether it stepped up, down, or stayed. Intervals are just named distances. C to D is a major second. C to E is a major third. C to G is a perfect fifth, the same leap as the opening of Twinkle Twinkle.

You do not memorize those labels first. You hear the motion. The name is a handle so you can talk about what you already noticed.

A ten-minute session that works

  1. Warm up with two notes you already know. Replay freely.
  2. Do a short mixed set — notes, then one octave question, then find a note on guitar.
  3. Stop while you are still accurate. Tired guessing teaches guessing.

What to practice after the first pair

  • Note recognition — hear a pitch, name the letter
  • One note, many pitches — the same letter at different heights
  • Low / middle / high — octave as its own question
  • Find it on guitar — tap a fret that matches what you heard
  • Intervals and short public-domain phrases
  • Western names, solfège, or sargam, from Settings

Common mistakes

Watching the answer more than you listen. Using only a sine wave when you play guitar — switch the instrument to acoustic or electric so the timbre matches the instrument in your hands. Adding three new notes in one evening. Skipping the guitar find step: naming a note and placing it on a string are related, and the second one is how songs leave the headphones and reach the fretboard.
