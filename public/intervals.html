Intervals by ear

By Vaibhav Verma · Updated 14 September 2026

An interval is the distance between two pitches. You do not start by reciting a chart. You start by hearing whether the second note went up, down, or stayed.

Practice intervals in the trainer

Motion before names

Play C, then E. The second sound is higher. That fact is already useful. The name “major third” is a handle for a motion you can already hear. If you skip the motion and only memorize labels, you will pass a quiz and still miss the leap in a song.

Earstep’s first interval work is that seed: higher, lower, or the same. Only after that do we add a few named distances, one pair at a time.

A few distances worth owning

From C, a major second is D — a small step, the neighbor. A major third is E — the brighter third of a major chord. A perfect fifth is G — the open leap at the start of Twinkle Twinkle. Those three show up constantly on guitar. You do not need a minor ninth on week one.

Sing the first note, then the second, even badly. The voice forces a decision. Then name the distance. Then, if you hold a guitar, find both pitches. Two frets that match the leap teach more than a flashcard.

Same letter, different job

Interval training is not the same as “name this isolated note.” You are allowed a reference. The first sound is the home. The second is the trip. Relative pitch is this skill. Absolute pitch is naming a lone beep with no home. You can play songs for a lifetime with relative pitch and never develop the rare absolute kind.

How this sits in the Journey

Earstep unlocks interval work after you can name a small set of letters and tell low from high. That is deliberate. If C and D are still a coin flip, a “major second” question is just another coin flip with fancier words.

When a pair is weak, practice only that pair. C to E until the third is obvious. Then C to G. Mixing every interval too early is the twelve-note problem in a new costume.

A short drill

  1. Hear two notes. Say up, down, or same before you name anything.
  2. If you know the first letter, guess the second from the motion.
  3. Check on the trainer, then find both notes on guitar once.
